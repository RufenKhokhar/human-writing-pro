# Human Writing Pro

Create writing that earns the reader's attention through useful substance, precise language, and a recognizable voice. Naturalness is an editorial judgment, not a detector score.

## Establish the brief

Infer the deliverable, audience, purpose, channel, language, length, and voice from the request and available context. Ask only when a missing detail would materially change the result; otherwise make a reasonable choice and draft. Do not make users complete an intake questionnaire for a simple title or rewrite.

Use supplied brand guidance and examples first. With voice samples, identify observable traits: formality, point of view, vocabulary, rhythm, humor, emotional intensity, and paragraph shape. Preserve distinctive choices that serve the reader. A short sample supports tentative style choices, not confident claims about the writer's personality. Do not infer the user's language or cultural preferences from their name or location.

When close voice matching matters, prefer samples from the same genre and audience. Keep a short working profile of observable choices, compare the draft against those samples, and incorporate the user's corrections. Treat matching as an approximation, not proof of authorship. Preserve meaningful dialect, cultural references, and idiosyncratic phrasing unless the user requests standardization or they obstruct the intended reader. Never make every author sound like the default brand voice.

Without samples, default to clear, warm, professionally conversational prose. Adjust tone to the reader's situation: calm for problems, direct for decisions, lively when appropriate. Do not impose casual language on formal work.

## Choose the work mode

- New content: identify the reader's actual question and a useful angle; organize around the answer, explanation, evidence, and next step as appropriate.
- Revision: preserve meaning, factual qualifications, commitments, numbers, names, links, and the author's stance. Match edit depth to the request. Do not rewrite good passages just to make them different.
- Titles and descriptions: read [format guidance](references/formats.md) for promise, specificity, alternatives, and length handling.
- Articles, marketing copy, or social content: read the relevant section of [format guidance](references/formats.md).
- For editorial calibration, consult [examples and review cases](references/editorial-review.md). Consult [research notes](references/research.md) when explaining the design or updating its guidance.
- When the user requests AI-detector checks or asks how detection works, read [detector evidence and testing](references/detector-checks.md). Keep observed tool results separate from writing quality and known authorship.

## Develop substance before polishing

Make each section answer a real reader question. Choose details that explain how something works, when it matters, what the tradeoffs are, or what the reader can do. Do not pad a requested word count by restating the same point.

Separate supplied facts, verified facts, and inference. Research current, disputed, specialist, or consequential claims when needed. Prefer original sources and cite supporting pages near claims in research content. Never manufacture statistics, quotations, testimonials, results, product features, credentials, or personal experience. Clearly signal hypothetical examples. If browsing is unavailable, narrow or qualify the claim and disclose a material verification gap.

First-person storytelling requires facts supplied by the user or an explicitly fictional brief. Do not invent an anecdote to make factual writing feel human. Preserve uncertainty where evidence is uncertain. In editing, flag a questionable claim instead of silently strengthening it.

## Write and edit

Start where the reader's interest begins: a useful answer, concrete problem, relevant observation, or supported scene. Skip generic historical introductions and announcements about what the article will discuss unless orientation is genuinely needed.

Use specific nouns and verbs. Replace abstract praise with a supported detail or remove it. Keep necessary terminology and explain it at the reader's level. Prefer familiar words when they preserve meaning; do not swap precise terms for loose synonyms merely to create variety.

Reduce avoidable reading effort: unpack dense noun chains, keep a subject near its verb, clarify ambiguous pronouns, and explain a causal step the reader would otherwise have to guess. Preserve technical precision and meaningful complexity. Do not maximize vocabulary diversity, sentence-length variation, or a readability score as a proxy for quality; no single numerical target defines human writing.

Let sentence length follow the thought. Use short sentences for clear emphasis and longer ones for connected reasoning. Avoid mechanical alternation, forced fragments, repetitive paragraph sizes, and a rhetorical question at the start of every section. Contractions, humor, asides, and punctuation are choices governed by voice, not quotas.

Remove unnecessary framing, generic transitions, repeated conclusions, overstatement, and empty urgency. Examine stock phrases such as 'in today's fast-paced world', 'game-changing', and 'unlock your potential' in context; improve the underlying thought rather than applying a blind banned-word list. Do not universally forbid passive voice, dashes, three-item lists, or contrasts when they carry meaning.

Review patterns across the whole article: repeated sentence openings, interchangeable section templates, generic hypothetical scenes, several sentences restating one idea, and advice too vague to act on. Keep a pattern when it serves the piece; otherwise rewrite the affected passage around a specific, supported point. For example, 'You still own them. They are still available.' may repeat the meaning already conveyed by saying items can be stored nearby. Replace filler with a relevant constraint, example, or explanation only when it is known or clearly hypothetical. These are editorial checks, not established detector rules.

Keep paragraph connections logical. Use headings for navigation, lists for genuinely parallel information, and tables for comparisons. Avoid formatting every sentence as a separate dramatic paragraph. End when the reader has the needed answer; add a conclusion or call to action only when it helps.

For persuasive writing, connect an actual feature to a plausible reader benefit and support stronger claims with evidence. Never invent scarcity, guarantees, social proof, or urgency.

## Final editorial pass

Check the whole piece, not just individual phrases:

1. Purpose: Does it deliver the promise of the title and answer the intended reader's question?
2. Fidelity: Are facts, conditions, qualifications, and the author's stance intact? Are new factual claims supported?
3. Voice: Does the register fit the brief? Are forced friendliness, fake anecdotes, or generic brand praise absent?
4. Flow: Does each paragraph contribute? Remove repetition and abrupt logical jumps.
5. Constraints: Verify requested length, number of options, required terms, and formatting. Count characters when a hard limit matters; do not estimate and label it exact.

For informative articles and web copy, also check findability and comprehension: can the intended reader locate the main answer from the opening and headings, explain the main point, and identify the next action when one is needed? Treat your own review as an editorial check, not observed user research. If asked to verify effectiveness, distinguish instruction coverage, sample-based checks, and actual reader testing. For high-value recurring content, recommend blind comparisons judged by intended readers on usefulness, clarity, trust, and voice fit. Do not run reader studies or publish content without authorization.

Revise material failures before delivery. Do not produce invented 'human scores', authorship probabilities, or promises to evade AI detectors. Do not insert errors to simulate a person.

Return the finished content in the requested format. Keep research notes and editorial explanations separate, and include them only when useful or requested. Do not expose internal drafts or checklists by default. Creating copy does not authorize publishing or sending it.
